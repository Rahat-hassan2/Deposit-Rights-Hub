Security Deposit Laws by State (2026): Return Deadlines, Limits & Tenant Rights

Moving into a new rental home is exciting, but getting your security deposit back when you move out can sometimes become a challenge. Many tenants wonder how much a landlord can legally charge, how long they have to return the money, and what to do if a deposit is withheld unfairly.

Understanding security deposit laws by state in 2026 can help renters protect their money, understand their rights, and prepare for a smoother move-out process.

Deposit rules differ across the United States. Each state has its own requirements regarding security deposit limits, refund deadlines, permitted deductions, written notices, and potential penalties for unlawful withholding.

In this guide, we explain how security deposits work, what tenants should check before moving out, and how online tools such as a security deposit calculator can help estimate a possible refund.

What Is a Security Deposit?

A security deposit is money a tenant pays to a landlord before moving into a rental property. It is generally intended to protect the landlord against certain financial losses, such as unpaid rent or damage beyond ordinary wear and tear.

A security deposit is not automatically a nonrefundable fee. Whether a landlord can keep any portion of it depends on the lease agreement and the applicable state or local law.

Common reasons a landlord may be allowed to deduct money include:

Unpaid rent, where permitted by law.
Repair costs for tenant-caused damage beyond ordinary wear and tear.
Certain unpaid charges authorized by the lease and applicable law.
Other specifically permitted expenses under local regulations.

Normal aging, routine use, and ordinary wear and tear are treated differently from damage in many jurisdictions. Tenants should review their state's specific rules before accepting deductions.

Security Deposit Laws by State in 2026

One of the most important things to understand is that there is no single security deposit law that applies identically across all 50 states.

Some states limit the amount a landlord can collect upfront. Others do not impose a general statutory cap. States also differ in how quickly landlords must return deposits after a tenancy ends.

For example, published state-law comparisons identify California's general deposit cap as one month's rent under its current rules, while New York generally limits residential security deposits to one month's rent. Other states have different limits, exceptions, and requirements.

These examples illustrate why renters should check the law for the state where their rental property is located rather than rely on general advice.

When reviewing your state's security deposit laws, check:

Maximum security deposit: The amount a landlord may legally collect.
Return deadline: The time allowed for returning the deposit or providing required notices.
Permitted deductions: The expenses that can legally be deducted.
Itemized statement: Whether the landlord must provide a written breakdown of deductions.
Interest requirements: Whether the deposit must earn interest under applicable law.
Penalties: Potential consequences for unlawful withholding.

Always verify the current statute and any relevant local requirements before taking legal action.

How Long Does a Landlord Have to Return a Security Deposit?

One of the most frequently asked questions among renters is: How long does a landlord have to return a security deposit?

The answer depends on the state, the rental agreement, and the circumstances surrounding the end of the tenancy.

Some jurisdictions have relatively short deadlines, while others allow more time. Certain laws also distinguish between returning a deposit with no deductions and providing notice when a landlord intends to withhold money.

To determine your deadline, identify your property's state and review the applicable law. Also check whether the deadline depends on when you vacate, return possession, provide a forwarding address, or satisfy another legal requirement.

What If Your Landlord Misses the Deadline?

If your landlord has not returned your deposit within the applicable deadline, take the following steps:

Review your lease and identify the relevant state security deposit law.
Confirm the move-out date and any notice requirements.
Gather your payment records, lease, move-in photographs, and move-out evidence.
Request the deposit or a written explanation of deductions.
If the dispute remains unresolved, contact a local tenant-rights organization or seek qualified legal advice about your options.

Some states provide remedies or penalties for unlawful withholding, but eligibility depends on the specific law and facts. A missed deadline does not necessarily produce the same result in every state.

What Can a Landlord Legally Deduct From a Security Deposit?

Understanding permitted deductions is essential when calculating your expected security deposit refund.

Depending on the applicable law and lease, deductions may include unpaid rent, qualifying property damage, or certain other authorized charges.

However, landlords generally cannot treat a security deposit as unrestricted money. Requirements concerning documentation, notice, and ordinary wear and tear vary by jurisdiction.

Examples of issues tenants should examine include:

Damage to walls, doors, flooring, or fixtures beyond normal wear.
Unpaid rent or other legally recoverable amounts.
Cleaning expenses, where a deduction is permitted under the applicable rules.
Missing keys or damaged items, when the charge is legally justified.

Ask for an itemized statement when appropriate, and compare each deduction with your lease and state law. Keep copies of receipts, inspection reports, photographs, and written communications.

How to Calculate Your Security Deposit Refund

A security deposit calculator can help renters estimate how much money they may receive after moving out.

A basic estimate starts with the original deposit and subtracts potential deductions.

Estimated refund = Original security deposit − Valid deductions

For example, suppose a tenant paid a $1,500 security deposit. The landlord proposes $150 for qualifying repairs and $100 for another legally permitted expense.

The estimated refund would be:

Original deposit: $1,500
Potential deductions: $250
Estimated refund: $1,250

This is only an illustration, not a determination that either deduction is legally valid. The final amount depends on the evidence, the lease, and the applicable law.

Using a security deposit calculator can make the arithmetic easier, but a calculator cannot independently establish whether a landlord's deductions are lawful.

How to Protect Your Security Deposit Before Moving Out

A little preparation can help prevent disagreements and make it easier to challenge incorrect deductions.

1. Document the Property's Condition

Take clear photographs and videos of each room before moving in and again when moving out. Keep dated records when possible.

2. Review Your Lease Agreement

Check the provisions covering security deposits, cleaning, damage, move-out inspections, notice, and the return of keys.

3. Follow the Required Move-Out Process

Provide any required notice, return the keys, and follow applicable inspection procedures. Keep written proof of important steps.

4. Provide a Forwarding Address

Where required, give your landlord a reliable forwarding address in the form and manner prescribed by local law.

5. Keep All Communication in Writing

Save emails, messages, receipts, inspection reports, and any itemized statement of deductions.

6. Understand Your Legal Options

If your landlord refuses to return money you believe is owed, review the applicable law and consider contacting a tenant advocacy organization or qualified attorney.

Why State-Specific Tenant Rights Matter

Two renters facing similar situations may have different legal options because their properties are located in different states.

For instance, the maximum deposit, return deadline, documentation requirements, and available remedies may differ substantially between jurisdictions.

This is why searching for general information about security deposits is only the first step. The most useful next step is to identify the law that applies to your particular rental property.

Tenants should prioritize official state statutes, government housing resources, and qualified local legal assistance when a dispute involves significant money or a potential court claim.

Frequently Asked Questions About Security Deposit Laws

Can a landlord keep my entire security deposit?

A landlord may retain some or all of a deposit when permitted by the lease and applicable law, depending on valid deductions and the amount owed. An entire deposit should not automatically be treated as forfeited simply because a tenant moves out.

Can I get my security deposit back if I break my lease?

Possibly. The outcome depends on the lease, applicable law, unpaid rent, qualifying damages, and any rules governing early termination. Breaking a lease does not automatically determine the exact amount a landlord may retain.

Do all states have a maximum security deposit limit?

No. Some states impose statutory caps, while others do not have a general cap. Additional exceptions or local requirements may apply.

What should I do if my landlord refuses to return my deposit?

Review your state's law, collect evidence, request an explanation in writing, and consider sending a written demand if appropriate. A local tenant organization or attorney can help you understand possible remedies.

Is an online security deposit calculator accurate?

A calculator can provide a useful estimate when its inputs and assumptions are correct. It should not be treated as a substitute for checking state law or determining whether disputed deductions are legally valid.

Where can I check security deposit laws for my state?

Start with the relevant state statute or an official government housing resource. You can also use a state-specific security deposit guide or calculator as a starting point, then verify important legal details against authoritative sources.
